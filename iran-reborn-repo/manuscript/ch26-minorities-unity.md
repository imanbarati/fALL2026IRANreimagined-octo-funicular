# Chapter 26 — Minorities and the Unity of Iran

Iran is not a single ethno-linguistic block. Kurds, Azeris, Baluch, Arabs of Khuzestan, Turkmen, Lurs, Gilakis, Mazanderanis, and smaller communities have lived within the same political borders for generations. Religious minorities — Sunni Muslims, Baha’is, Christians of several traditions, Jews, Zoroastrians, and others — have endured parallel histories of recognition, restriction, and, at times, persecution. A recovery that treats “Iranian” as synonymous with one language, one sect, or one historical narrative will reproduce the fractures that previous regimes exploited. A recovery that dissolves the state into competing ethnic or religious fiefdoms will invite the civil-war scenarios that Chapter 2 and the Risks appendix already flag.

The coalition’s starting principle is equal citizenship under a single sovereign state that protects territorial integrity. How that principle is institutionalized — unitary, federal, or some intermediate form of strong local government — is a question for constitution-making, not for premature declaration. What cannot wait is the end of discriminatory practice and the beginning of inclusive institutions.

## The damage that must be undone

Decades of centralized control, security-first governance in the periphery, and official preference for Persian-language and Twelver Shi’a norms have left measurable deficits. Kurdish and Baluch regions have experienced higher rates of underdevelopment, heavier security presence, and recurrent cycles of protest and repression. Azeri cultural and linguistic rights have been constrained even while Azeris have been prominent in national politics and commerce. Arab communities in Khuzestan have faced environmental degradation, water stress, and political marginalization layered on top of economic neglect. Baha’is have been systematically excluded from higher education and public employment. Sunni communities have faced barriers to religious infrastructure and representation.

These are not ancient grievances alone. They are contemporary policy outcomes. War damage, sanctions, and the 2025–2026 crises have hit peripheral provinces hard; recovery funds that flow only through old central channels will deepen the imbalance.

## Principles for a unitary state of equal citizens

1. **Equal citizenship, not hierarchical belonging.** No Iranian is a guest in the national project. Language, religion, and ethnicity are protected private and cultural rights; they are not tests of loyalty.
2. **Territorial integrity is non-negotiable.** Separatist projects, whether armed or political, threaten the recovery of everyone. The coalition will not trade border stability for any external promise of support.
3. **Language rights without linguistic fragmentation.** Persian remains the common language of national administration and inter-provincial communication. Mother-tongue education and cultural media in Kurdish, Azeri Turkish, Arabic, Baluchi, and other languages must be protected and resourced. The model is additive, not subtractive.
4. **Religious freedom without state religion.** The end of the Islamic Republic’s official doctrine does not mean the end of religion in public life. It means the end of state coercion in matters of belief, the end of second-class legal status for non-recognized communities, and the equal protection of all houses of worship and religious schools that respect the civil law.
5. **Security that does not substitute for development.** Heavy security deployments in minority regions must be reviewed, reduced where threat levels allow, and paired with transparent civilian oversight and accelerated infrastructure and job programs.

## Practical steps

In the first hundred days:

- Issue a public commitment to equal citizenship and the end of official discrimination on ethnic or religious grounds.
- Release prisoners held primarily for peaceful advocacy of cultural or linguistic rights.
- Open channels for minority community representatives (including women and youth) to participate in transitional advisory bodies.
- Begin audit of development spending and security budgets by province, with publication of the findings.

In the stabilization phase:

- Rewrite or repeal laws that impose second-class status on religious minorities.
- Expand mother-tongue schooling options while maintaining national curriculum standards in Persian.
- Design reconstruction projects (water, energy, transport, housing) with explicit equity metrics for historically neglected provinces.
- Establish independent complaint mechanisms for discrimination in employment, education, and public services.

On the larger constitutional question — federalism versus a reformed unitary state with strong provinces — the coalition will present both sides fairly in the constitution-making chapter. Federal arrangements can protect cultural rights and local accountability; they can also freeze ethnic competition and complicate national economic policy. Unitary arrangements can preserve cohesion and mobility; they can also reproduce central neglect. The decision belongs to a future constitutional process, not to any single faction’s blueprint.

## Objections and Honest Limits

*"Talk of minorities encourages separatism."* Silence about discrimination has not prevented unrest; it has radicalized it. Equal citizenship and cultural rights are the strongest long-term antidote to separatist appeal.

*"Federalism is the only solution."* Federalism is one possible institutional answer. It is not the only one, and it carries its own risks of paralysis and external interference. The coalition will not pre-empt the constitutional debate.

*"Religious minorities are too small to matter."* The principle of equal citizenship does not depend on headcount. Systematic exclusion of any community corrodes the legitimacy of the whole.

*"Security conditions in the periphery make rapid change impossible."* Security conditions are partly the product of past policy. Transparent development, political inclusion, and the end of collective punishment are themselves security measures.

## What We Will Do / What You Can Do

We will treat minority inclusion as a cross-cutting test of every recovery policy. We will maintain open channels with civil-society groups from all communities and will reject any transitional arrangement that freezes old hierarchies.

If you belong to a minority community: document discrimination, organize across ethnic and religious lines where possible, and demand seats at the table rather than parallel tables. If you are from the Persian-speaking majority: treat equal citizenship as a non-negotiable condition of the new order, not as a concession. If you are a policymaker: measure reconstruction by equity across provinces and communities, not only by aggregate national numbers.

Unity that rests on the subordination of any community is temporary. Unity that rests on equal citizenship is the only kind worth recovering.

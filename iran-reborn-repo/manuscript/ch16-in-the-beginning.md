# Chapter 16 — In the Beginning: How the Gods Were Written

*A word before we begin.* This chapter examines how deities are portrayed in ancient and sacred texts, especially the way they are said to choose and punish their targets. It is literary and historical analysis, not mockery. The coalition includes believers, doubters and unbelievers, and the book is addressed to all of them. We do not claim to settle whether any god exists. Nietzsche's line that "God is dead" was a diagnosis of a culture, not a proof; we use it the same way: the claim that an old picture of divine authority has lost its hold on many people, in Iran and beyond. Here we ask what that old picture said.

## The oldest stories: gods who destroy

Long before the Bible and the Quran, Mesopotamian myths described gods who destroyed humanity out of irritation. In the Atrahasis tradition, the gods send plague, drought and finally a flood because human noise disturbs their rest; one god warns a human hero, who survives. The Hebrew flood story in Genesis 6–9 echoes this structure, but with a moral motive: the destruction is a response to human violence and corruption. Scholars generally see the Genesis account as a reworking of older regional traditions rather than an independent invention.

## The Hebrew Bible: a god who warns, chooses and strikes

The Hebrew Bible presents a God with several faces. Some passages show him sparing, pleading and forgiving; the book of Jonah ends with God sparing Nineveh, and Ezekiel 18 says God takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked. Others show him killing, and the pattern of **who is targeted** is worth examining:

1. **The corrupt or violent**: the generation of the flood (Genesis 6–9); Sodom and Gomorrah, destroyed over violence and cruelty to strangers (Genesis 19).
2. **Oppressors of the chosen people**: Pharaoh and Egypt through the plagues, ending with the death of firstborn children (Exodus 7–12), a collective punishment that many readers, ancient and modern, find hard to defend.
3. **Peoples standing in the way of the promise**: the commands to devote Canaanite cities to destruction (Deuteronomy 20; Joshua 6) and the order to destroy Amalek (1 Samuel 15).
4. **Challengers of leadership**: Korah and his followers, swallowed by the earth (Numbers 16).
5. **Those who violate sacred boundaries**: Uzzah, struck dead for steadying the ark (2 Samuel 6).
6. **Idolaters among the chosen**: the golden calf episode and its aftermath (Exodus 32).

Three observations follow. First, **warning usually precedes punishment** in the narratives, delivered by a prophet or a sign. Second, **punishments are often collective**, striking families, cities and peoples, a feature that modern moral intuitions about individual responsibility struggle with. Third, **the targets map neatly onto the community's own enemies and fears**, which is why historians suspect the texts also encode political memory.

## The historical question

Archaeologists and historians are largely skeptical that a sudden military conquest of Canaan happened as Joshua describes it, noting that the archaeological record suggests a gradual, mixed process `[VERIFY: scholarly consensus]`. Many scholars read the "devote to destruction" language as a stylized, hyperbolic war rhetoric common in the ancient Near East, comparable to inscriptions of other kings of the time. Others, including some believers, argue that the texts nonetheless deserve moral scrutiny because later generations used them as authority for real violence.

## The New Testament and the Quran

The New Testament tilts toward mercy and enemy-love, but it also contains stories of sudden divine judgment, such as the deaths of Ananias and Sapphira (Acts 5) and the vast imagery of Revelation.

The Quran retells the destruction of earlier peoples: 'Ad, Thamud, the people of Lot and the people of Madyan in Surah al-A'raf, Surah Hud and elsewhere. The pattern is distinctive: **a messenger is sent first, he warns, the people mock or reject him, and then destruction comes while the believers are saved.** The punishments are framed as consequences of persistent injustice and arrogance after repeated warning. The Quran also contains passages on fighting enemies, which Muslim scholars have interpreted in context for centuries; and verses that state that there is no compulsion in religion (2:256) and that killing one innocent person is like killing all humanity (5:32). Interpretation has mattered as much as the text: the same verses have been read as limits on war and as licenses for it.

## Iran's own older picture

Zoroastrianism, the pre-Islamic faith of Persia, drew a different line. Its supreme god, Ahura Mazda, is wholly good; destruction, disease and death belong to an opposing force, Angra Mainyu. The Zoroastrian answer to "why is there suffering?" is that a good god is not the author of evil. Whatever one believes, the Persian tradition shows that the idea of a god who kills his targets was never the only option available to the human imagination, and that it is part of Iran's own heritage to ask hard moral questions of the divine.

## How the picture was used

A deity who chooses and kills targets is a powerful political idea. Rulers, priests and movements across history have claimed to know whom the deity wants removed. That is the use that matters for this book. When a modern state claims divine authority for its security campaigns, its executions and its censors, it is borrowing the oldest justification there is (see Chapter 13). Believers have long argued about which parts of their scriptures are descriptive history, poetry, moral education or timeless command. A free society lets that argument happen in the open.

## What believers and critics say

- **Critics** argue that a god who orders the death of children cannot be perfectly good, and that treating such texts as divine invites the same behavior in humans. Some scholars add that the texts are plainly human projections of tribal power.
- **Believers and many theologians** respond that scripture records a gradual moral education of humanity; that violent passages are hyperbole, narrative or specific to their time; that God's judgment is just beyond human sight; or that the deepest message is mercy, which repeatedly overrules wrath in the texts themselves.
- **A third group**, within and outside religions, holds that scripture should be read as literature and moral argument, with the humane passages winning over the violent ones, and that the right to read it this way should be protected by law.

The coalition does not impose a reading. It defends the freedom to hold each of them.

## Objections and Honest Limits

*"This is blasphemy."* We write as analysts, not as judges of anyone's faith. Believers across traditions have asked these very questions about their own texts for centuries.

*"You picked the violent passages."* Yes, because the question was about targets and killing. The mercy passages are real and are cited above; a full treatment would give them equal weight.

*"You covered some religions and not others."* True. Hindu, Buddhist, Baha'i, Yazidi and other traditions have their own pictures of divine justice and violence and deserve their own chapter later.

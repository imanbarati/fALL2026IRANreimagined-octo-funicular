# Chapter 32 — The Diaspora Question

Millions of Iranians live outside the country’s borders. They include political exiles, economic migrants, students who stayed, professionals who left, and the children and grandchildren of earlier waves. They hold capital, skills, networks, and memory. They also hold competing visions of the future and competing claims to speak for those still inside. A recovery that ignores the diaspora wastes resources. A recovery that is designed and directed primarily from outside risks legitimacy failure and internal rejection.

## The dual reality

The diaspora is not a single actor. It contains monarchists, republicans, leftists, liberals, nationalists, and the apolitical. It contains those who left under threat and those who left for opportunity. It contains people who send remittances every month and people who have lost touch. Treating “the diaspora” as a unified lobby or a government-in-waiting is a category error.

At the same time, the aggregate resources are real: professional expertise in medicine, engineering, finance, and technology; entrepreneurial experience; access to international capital and institutions; and a living archive of what was lost and what might be rebuilt. Chapter 9 on reverse migration and Chapter 30 on universities already depend on this reservoir.

## Principles for a healthy relationship

1. **Inside primacy.** Final political decisions about the form of the state, the constitution, and the distribution of power belong to those who live under the new order. The diaspora can advise, fund, and staff; it cannot rule by remote control.
2. **No exile government by default.** An “exile government” that claims authority without a clear, time-limited mandate and without mechanisms of accountability to people inside the country will lack legitimacy and will invite factional competition. Transitional arrangements should be designed to transfer authority inward as quickly as security and logistics allow.
3. **Open channels, not parallel states.** Dual appointments, return packages, remote collaboration, and advisory roles are preferable to the creation of permanent parallel institutions that compete with domestic ones.
4. **Transparency of funding and influence.** Reconstruction money, political donations, and media operations that originate in the diaspora should be disclosed. Hidden financing breeds suspicion and capture.
5. **Inclusion across political lines.** No single diaspora faction should be treated as the sole interlocutor. A recovering Iran needs the skills of people who disagree with one another.

## Practical steps

- Create a formal, time-limited diaspora advisory mechanism attached to the transitional authority, with transparent membership and a clear end date or renewal process.
- Design return and circulation programs (see Chapters 9, 29, 30) that do not require permanent relocation or political allegiance tests.
- Establish clear rules for diaspora participation in reconstruction contracting and investment: beneficial ownership disclosure, competition, and anti-corruption safeguards apply equally.
- Support independent diaspora media and research that inform rather than monopolize the narrative.
- Avoid recognition of any single exile body as “the” representative of the Iranian people unless and until a domestic process confers that status.

## Objections and Honest Limits

*"The diaspora has the money and the freedom; it should lead."* Money and freedom are assets. Legitimacy is earned by those who share the daily risks and the daily consequences. Leadership that is perceived as external will be resisted.

*"Exiles are out of touch."* Some are. Many are not. The remedy is structured, two-way channels and a bias toward those who maintain real ties and who accept the primacy of domestic decision-making.

*"We need a government-in-waiting to coordinate."* Coordination is necessary. A self-appointed government-in-waiting is a different and riskier instrument. Prefer technical task forces and advisory councils with sunset clauses.

## What We Will Do / What You Can Do

We will treat the diaspora as a strategic asset under the principle of inside primacy. We will oppose both the exclusion of diaspora skills and the substitution of diaspora politics for domestic consent.

If you are in the diaspora: offer concrete skills and resources; disclose funding; accept that final authority rests inside; avoid presenting any single faction as the sole voice. If you are inside: create channels that can absorb diaspora capacity without surrendering control; judge contributions by competence and transparency, not by political label. If you design transitional institutions: write the rules for diaspora participation in advance, with clear limits and accountability.

The diaspora can help Iran recover. It cannot recover Iran by itself. The relationship must be built on that recognition.

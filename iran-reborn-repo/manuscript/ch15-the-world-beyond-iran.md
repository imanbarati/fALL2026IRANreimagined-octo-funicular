# Chapter 15 — The World Beyond Iran: Children in the Mines, Chinese on the Moon, and a Narrow Strait

An Iranian recovery plan is not written in a vacuum. It will be carried out in a world whose old order is wearing out and whose new order has not yet been agreed. This chapter looks at three snapshots of that world, because each one will shape what Iran can ask of the future and what it will be asked to give.

*Dates in this chapter are as of 1 November 2026 and come from news reports and agency statements; anything fast-moving is marked `[VERIFY]`.*

## Snapshot one: the children in the mines

The batteries that power phones, laptops and electric cars depend on cobalt, and the Democratic Republic of Congo supplies the great majority of the world's cobalt, with figures above 70 percent often cited. Between roughly 15 and 30 percent of Congolese cobalt comes from artisanal miners who dig by hand, often without protective gear `[VERIFY: share]`. In 2022 a congressional hearing heard testimony that about 40,000 children were working in such mines. Research from universities and NGOs describes children as young as six or seven sorting, washing and carrying ore, and reporting from Kolwezi in June 2026 still describes children on mining spoil heaps. The International Labour Organization's COTECCO project reported that it had taken over 9,000 children out of artisanal cobalt mining and into school or training by December 2024, which shows both that the problem is real and that it can be reduced.

Two things are true at once. Chinese firms dominate cobalt mining and refining in the Congo, and critics accuse some of weak oversight. Western and other companies buy the refined metal and put it in products sold worldwide, so the chain of responsibility runs through many countries, including consumers. Poverty is the engine: for many families, artisanal mining is the best income available.

**Why this matters for Iran.** Iran has its own children at work: street vending, waste collection, brick kilns, workshops and farms, many of them Afghan or from poor provinces `[VERIFY]`. Industrial safety is also a national wound; mining disasters in recent years have killed dozens of workers `[VERIFY]`. A reborn Iran should not only ask the world to behave better. It should prove that its own supply chains, mines and factories meet the standard it asks of others.

## Snapshot two: Chinese on the Moon

China has set a goal of landing astronauts on the Moon by 2030. In May 2026 it merged its robotic Chang'e program with its crewed lunar program under one Lunar Exploration Project. Its next robotic mission, Chang'e-7, was meant to search for water ice at the south pole; on 23 August 2026 China announced it could not launch within the window planned for this year, and reports suggest the delay could run into 2027 `[VERIFY: new date]`. Weather has been mentioned as one factor, but the official statement said only that launch conditions were not met.

The Moon matters to a recovery book for a simple reason: the twenty-first-century contest is about who masters energy, materials, launch capacity and data. Space is the visible edge of a broader race in batteries, chips, rare earths and artificial intelligence. A country that cannot keep its lights on cannot join that race. The distance between Iran's rebuilt power plants and a lunar base is enormous; the lesson is that technological ambition grows out of boring things, such as stable electricity, honest accounting, open universities and trusted institutions.

## Snapshot three: the Strait of Hormuz

About a fifth of the world's oil has traditionally passed through the Strait of Hormuz, a channel only a few miles wide in each direction. Reporting from 2026 describes this sequence:

- After the US and Israel began operations against Iran in February 2026, Tehran closed the strait, and oil prices rose.
- On 8 April a two-week ceasefire and a reopening were agreed; a few days later the US announced a blockade of access to Iranian ports, and Iran reopened and then fired on vessels within days.
- By October, reports described a record week of attacks on commercial vessels (twelve in the week to 11 October), traffic tracked by IMF PortWatch at a tiny fraction of the pre-crisis baseline of about 85 transits per day, and Brent crude around 103 to 105 dollars. Other sources report different transit counts, including US-facilitated convoys averaging around 30 a day, and some web pages assert the strait is "open". **The picture is contested and changes daily; verify before citing.**
- On 31 October Iran's foreign minister said Tehran wants recognition of one shipping corridor agreed with Oman, with other routes closed, and linked reopening to talks with Washington.

The strait is Iran's greatest leverage and its greatest trap. Leverage, because a closed strait raises prices worldwide and brings the world to the table. A trap, because every closure destroys the trade, insurance, oil revenue and goodwill that reconstruction depends on, and it hands rivals a reason to build pipelines, ports and alternatives that bypass Iran for good. A sustainable settlement must treat free passage as a shared interest of Iran, its Gulf neighbors, Oman, Asian importers and the major powers, with transparent rules and a joint monitoring body.

## The old order is dead; the new one is unwritten

For three decades after the Cold War, a single power set many of the rules of trade, shipping and security. That order is eroding. New centers of power, bruised alliances, trade wars and open fighting over chokepoints mean that many states are hedging. In this world, small and middle powers must do three things: avoid being anyone's battlefield, diversify partners (Chapter 10), and build domestic strength.

For Iran, the point is this: **neither the old order nor any new patron will rescue it.** Reconstruction will be paid for by Iranians working under rules they can trust, with partners who are chosen openly.

## Objections and Honest Limits

*"These three stories have nothing to do with each other."* They are connected by one thread: who bears the cost of other people's power. Children bear the cost of cheap batteries, sailors bear the cost of closed straits, and citizens bear the cost of space and security races.

*"Iran should use Hormuz as leverage."* Many strategists in and out of Iran say so. The coalition's view is that leverage that wrecks your own economy is a weak currency; but this is a debate with serious defenders on both sides.

*"The sources are inconsistent."* Yes. That is why the dates, numbers and `[VERIFY]` tags are in the text.

# H02 — 1989–1997: Reconstruction, succession and the security economy

## Rebuilding after a devastating war
The end of the Iran–Iraq War created an urgent practical task: rebuild damaged cities, transport, energy infrastructure and industry; reintegrate veterans; restore trade; and address a population exhausted by years of mobilization. Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani's presidency, beginning in 1989, presented reconstruction as a national priority. The government pursued infrastructure investment, state planning and a measure of economic opening.

Reconstruction was real and necessary. The question is how the distribution of contracts, credit, assets and political influence shaped the state that emerged. When emergency reconstruction is channelled through institutions with limited public scrutiny, the result can be both physical recovery and a stronger network of insiders.

## Formal ministries and parallel institutions
The Islamic Republic's economy cannot be understood as a simple state-versus-market contest. Alongside ministries and ordinary public companies stood religious foundations (bonyads), military-linked contractors, pension funds, cooperatives and entities connected to powerful offices. Some performed welfare and charitable functions; others became large corporate actors with privileged access to assets, finance or procurement.

Research by Manochehr Dorraj on bonyads describes their evolution from charitable institutions into major instruments of economic and political power, operating in parallel with formal bureaucracy. Kevan Harris's work on pseudo-privatization argues that the transfer of public assets did not necessarily create competitive private ownership: assets could move instead to parastatal bodies, foundations and military-linked firms. This complicates the idea that privatization automatically means liberalization.

## A presidency with influence, not sovereignty
Rafsanjani's government could shape economic policy, but the Supreme Leader and unelected institutions retained strategic authority. The presidency's room for manoeuvre was conditional. Elected officials could be held responsible for inflation, unemployment or social frustration while lacking full control over institutions that set major political and security boundaries.

This separation of responsibility from authority became politically consequential. Citizens were asked to judge governments through elections, yet the range of permissible change remained constrained. The result was not the absence of politics, but a system in which accountability and actual power did not line up neatly.

## The unresolved social contract
Reconstruction promised normal life after war. Yet economic modernization did not produce an equivalent opening in political rights. Inflation, inequality, uneven access to opportunity and the power of privileged institutions fed criticism. The regime could point to national rebuilding and public services; critics could point to political exclusion and lack of transparency.

Two simplifications should be avoided. Not every economic failure was caused by one institution: war damage, demographic pressures, oil-price cycles and international constraints mattered. Nor was every foundation or contractor identical. The issue is the absence of transparent rules, beneficial-ownership disclosure, independent audit and equal access to public procurement.

## Why it matters for 2026
The reconstruction period offers a warning for any future transition. Replacing one set of political patrons with another, or transferring public assets without competition and audit, can reproduce the same problem under new names. Recovery requires public registers of assets, independent procurement review, conflict-of-interest rules, publication of state-company accounts and parliamentary oversight of security-linked commercial activity.

## Objections and honest limits
The reconstruction period also delivered infrastructure and economic growth in difficult circumstances. A balanced history must measure outcomes rather than assume every project was corrupt. It must distinguish documented ownership and contracts from allegations about individual beneficiaries.

## What we will do / what readers can do
Build an open register of public assets and major contracts; preserve privatization decrees and company filings; and insist that post-conflict reconstruction be audited by independent institutions.

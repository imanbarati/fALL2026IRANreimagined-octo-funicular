# Chapter 1 — The Ledger of Damage

A recovery begins with an inventory. Before anyone promises a new Iran, someone has to count what is broken. This chapter is the coalition's first attempt at that count. It is deliberately split into two ledgers, because the people of Iran have been hit twice: by war, and by the way they have been governed.

## The first ledger: the war

Reporting in 2026 describes a six-month US-Israeli war on Iran and a recovery that is already being fought over before the rubble is cleared. Analysts note that Tehran's factions hold competing visions: a government that wants trade, oil revenue and diplomacy, and security institutions that want to rebuild deterrence first. Photographs from Tehran in June 2026 show machinery working beside damaged residential buildings. Gulf states, meanwhile, are reported to be reluctant to fund a reconstruction bill estimated near 300 billion dollars `[VERIFY]`.

Each category of war damage needs its own line in the ledger:

- **Homes and neighborhoods.** Who lost a roof? Who is displaced?
- **Infrastructure.** Power plants, substations, water treatment, bridges, ports, rail, fuel depots, telecoms.
- **Health and education.** Hospitals, clinics, schools, universities, laboratories.
- **Industry and the bazaar.** Factories, warehouses, small workshops, supply chains.
- **Heritage.** Mosques, churches, synagogues, museums, libraries, historic quarters.
- **People.** The dead, the wounded, the disappeared, the traumatized.

The coalition's first demand is simple: **count publicly, count independently, and publish the counts.** A regime that hides its dead cannot rebuild honestly.

## The second ledger: decades of misrule

The war did not hit a healthy country. Years of inflation, a collapsing currency, capital flight, corruption and sanctions-driven isolation had already hollowed out wages and savings. Figures circulating in 2026 describe inflation near 90 percent, food inflation above 100 percent, and a rial worth a fraction of what it was a year earlier `[VERIFY: all figures]`. Whatever the exact number, families describe spending most of their income on food.

On top of the economy sits a second kind of damage that appears in no budget: the damage to trust. Citizens learned that official statistics lie, that courts follow orders, that protest is met with bullets, and that the internet can be shut off at will. The 2022 and 2025–26 uprisings were answered with force; counts of the dead differ enormously between government and independent sources `[VERIFY]`. A country in which the truth is a state secret cannot rebuild, because rebuilding requires every citizen to believe a signature means something.

## The third ledger: what leaves

Some of the largest losses are people. Doctors, nurses, engineers, scientists and students are leaving in large numbers. Chapter 9 takes up this exodus in full. For now, note that this is the only loss that compounds: every departed teacher is a classroom that produces fewer graduates for twenty years.

## What the ledger is for

The ledger is not a lament. It has three uses:

1. **Priority.** Money and time are finite; the ledger tells us whether water or power, hospitals or housing, comes first.
2. **Accountability.** Damage caused by negligence or corruption is different from damage caused by war, and the remedies differ.
3. **Hope.** A counted loss can be repaired. An uncounted loss only festers.

## Objections and Honest Limits

*"The numbers are impossible to get."* Largely true today. That is why the ledger begins with open-source satellite imagery, independent surveys, diaspora and domestic volunteer networks, and with a legal demand for access once any transitional authority exists.

*"Blaming the regime ignores the foreign attackers."* The ledger counts both. Responsibility for war damage is a matter for international law; responsibility for decades of misrule is a matter for Iranians. Nothing here excuses either.

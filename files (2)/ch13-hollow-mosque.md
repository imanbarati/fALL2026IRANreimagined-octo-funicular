# Chapter 13 — The Hollowed Mosque: Religious Control and the Death of the Congregation

A faith is a living thing. It breathes in congregations: families praying together, neighbors gathering, scholars arguing, charity flowing between people who know each other. This chapter argues that when a state takes over religion, it can hollow out the very faith it claims to protect, and with it the wider habit of gathering freely.

## What control looks like

Iranians report, and independent scholars have documented, an architecture of control that touches many parts of life: compulsory religious content in schools, official clerical bodies appointed by the state, restrictions on dress, surveillance of gatherings, vetting of preachers, and punishment for religious dissent, conversion and unorthodox belief. Members of minority faiths, including Baha'is, Christians, Sunnis, Jews, Sufis and others, face discrimination of varying severity. The coalition does not compare Iran to a fictional dystopia lightly; we describe the pattern as one in which religion is turned from a private conscience into a public instrument of administration.

## The hollowing effect

Opinion surveys by independent researchers inside and outside Iran suggest a sharp drift away from official religion, with large shares of respondents describing themselves as non-religious, spiritual-but-not-religious, or following other faiths `[VERIFY: GAMAAN and other surveys; sampling and methodology caveats]`. Mosque attendance outside state-organized occasions is widely reported to have declined. None of this proves that Iranians have abandoned faith. It suggests they are abandoning a state-run version of it.

For believers, this is a tragedy too: pious families see their faith associated in the public mind with coercion and corruption. Religious leaders who criticized state control have been silenced.

## The civic cost

Congregations are the original civil society. When all gatherings need permission, citizens lose practice in organizing, deliberating and trusting one another. A parliament of the people grows from many such habits. Control of mosques, charities, guilds, theater, sports and neighborhood associations produces a country of individuals who cannot cooperate except under orders.

## What the coalition proposes

1. **Freedom of conscience.** Every Iranian may believe, practice, change or leave a faith, or have none, without penalty.
2. **Separation of religious institutions from state power.** Clerics may speak, teach and organize, as any citizen, but no religious body holds legal authority over the state, courts, army or economy.
3. **Equal protection for all faiths**, including minority communities, with restoration of seized property, houses of worship and cemeteries.
4. **Independent religious institutions.** Seminaries, mosques and charities run by their communities, with transparent finances.
5. **Neutral schools.** Teaching about religions as culture and history, with optional confessional classes outside the core curriculum.
6. **Freedom of assembly.** A simple notification system replaces permission for gatherings, associations, congregations and festivals.
7. **Honor to the sacred.** Protection of shrines, mosques and holy sites from politics and from damage.

## Objections and Honest Limits

*"This is an attack on Islam."* It is not. It is a defense of the right to practice Islam, or any faith or none, free of coercion. Many devout Muslims and senior clerics have made similar arguments.

*"Secularism will offend the religious majority."* Many believers favor it because they have seen what state control does to their faith. A sizable portion of society still supports a role for religion in public life; the coalition respects that view and proposes that it be argued and voted on freely, not enforced.

*"The survey numbers are unreliable."* We agree they carry big uncertainties, especially online surveys in a repressive setting. We cite them as indications, not certainties.

# Chapter 27 — The IRGC Economy

No recovery of the Iranian economy is possible while the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps and its affiliated networks retain their interlocking control of major industries, trade channels, construction, finance, and smuggling routes. The problem is not merely that a military organization has commercial interests. The problem is that those interests have been protected by force, opaque ownership, preferential access to foreign exchange, exemption from ordinary regulation, and the ability to crowd out or absorb private competitors. The result is a distorted economy in which political loyalty and security connections substitute for productivity, and in which ordinary firms face a permanently tilted field.

Post-war reconstruction that simply redirects contracts to the same networks will not rebuild Iran. It will recreate the same capture under new flags. The Al Jazeera analysis of August 2026 already warned that a recovery dominated by IRGC-linked organizations would shape the direction of the post-war state itself. Gulf hesitation to fund large-scale reconstruction is partly a bet on exactly that outcome. A civilian, transparent alternative must be ready.

## Mapping the capture

The IRGC’s economic footprint is not a single holding company. It is a web: construction and infrastructure giants, oil and petrochemical interests, telecommunications and technology, banking and credit institutions, import-export platforms, and informal or semi-formal trade that benefits from border control and sanctions-evasion expertise. Some entities operate under formal names; others use layered ownership, bonyads, and cooperative structures that obscure beneficial control. Preferential access to hard currency, government contracts, and regulatory forbearance has compounded the advantage over decades.

War and sanctions have not dismantled this web; in some sectors they have tightened it, because the same networks that can move goods under restriction also control the scarcity rents. Any transitional authority that ignores this structure will find that “private sector revival” becomes a slogan while the commanding heights remain in the same hands.

## Principles for dismantling without chaos

1. **Lustration with limits.** Individuals with documented responsibility for violent repression or large-scale corruption should be barred from public office and from controlling systemic economic assets. Broad, class-based purges of every person who ever held a mid-level post in an IRGC-linked firm risk repeating the de-Baathification error: emptying institutions of competence and creating a pool of resentful, skilled opponents. Vetting must be individual, evidence-based, and subject to appeal.
2. **Transparency before transfer.** Before any major asset is privatized or restructured, beneficial ownership must be published, liabilities assessed, and worker claims protected. Fire-sale privatizations to connected insiders or to the highest foreign bidder without safeguards will produce oligarchs, not markets.
3. **Competition, not replacement monopolies.** The goal is not to hand IRGC assets to a new set of political favorites. It is to open sectors to genuine entry, break exclusive licenses, and subject remaining state or quasi-state holdings to ordinary commercial law and independent regulation.
4. **Worker and community stakes.** Employees and local communities that have depended on these enterprises for livelihoods must have a voice in restructuring and a share in any upside. Abrupt closures without transition support will turn economic reform into a security problem.
5. **International coordination without foreign capture.** Sanctions relief and reconstruction finance should be sequenced to reward verified steps toward transparency and competition. They should not become a vehicle for external powers to acquire strategic assets on preferential terms.

## Sequencing in the emergency and stabilization phases

**Emergency (first six months):**

- Freeze major new contract awards to opaque IRGC-linked entities pending ownership disclosure.
- Install independent auditors and monitors in systemic firms (banking, energy, telecom, large construction).
- Protect continuity of essential services (power, fuel, basic construction) so that reform does not equal blackout or unemployment spike.
- Publish a first-cut map of the largest holdings and their known affiliations.

**Stabilization:**

- Enact a beneficial-ownership registry with criminal penalties for false disclosure.
- Begin case-by-case restructuring: some firms can be cleaned and listed; some can be broken up; some must be wound down.
- Open previously closed sectors to new domestic and foreign entrants under clear, non-discriminatory rules.
- Create a specialized commercial court track for disputes arising from restructuring, with published decisions.
- Tie further sanctions relief and large reconstruction disbursements to verified milestones on ownership transparency and competition.

This sequencing draws on lessons from other post-authoritarian and post-conflict transitions: rapid, opaque transfers create new predators; endless delay protects the old ones. The middle path is published rules, independent oversight, and staged opening.

## Objections and Honest Limits

*"The IRGC is the only organization that can deliver large projects quickly."* Speed purchased by opacity and coercion is not a sustainable advantage. Competent private and civilian public contractors exist inside Iran and in the diaspora; the barrier has been access, not ability.

*"Attacking the IRGC economy will provoke armed resistance."* That risk is real and must be managed with security policy, not denied. The alternative — leaving the economic power structure intact — guarantees that any political transition remains reversible and that reconstruction rents continue to fund parallel power centers.

*"Privatization will just create new oligarchs."* It will, if done badly. Transparent auctions, employee ownership options, competition policy, and independent regulators are the difference between market creation and asset stripping.

*"Foreign investors will dominate."* Clear rules, local-content requirements where sensible, and a strong competition authority can prevent domination. Fear of foreign capital is not a reason to preserve domestic monopolies that have already extracted and underperformed.

## What We Will Do / What You Can Do

We will treat the dismantling of coercive economic monopolies as a core stabilization task, equal in priority to currency stability and basic service continuity. We will maintain a living annex of model disclosure laws, restructuring playbooks, and red-team risk assessments.

If you work inside an IRGC-linked enterprise: document ownership structures and irregularities where it is safe to do so; prepare for a future in which competence, not connection, determines survival. If you are a private entrepreneur: organize sector associations that can demand level rules and that can absorb released capacity. If you are in the diaspora with capital or expertise: condition participation on transparency milestones. If you design reconstruction finance: make beneficial-ownership disclosure and competition metrics gating conditions, not afterthoughts.

An economy that cannot tell who owns what, and that cannot let better firms win, cannot recover. The IRGC economy is not a side issue. It is the central structural obstacle. Removing it carefully is the work of national rejuvenation.

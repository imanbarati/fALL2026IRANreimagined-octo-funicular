# Chapter 17 — "Nobody Knows More Than Me": Power and the Claim of Secret Knowledge

Every age has leaders who say they know what others do not. This chapter takes one prominent contemporary example, US President Donald Trump, as a case study in how claims of unique knowledge work in politics, how they are checked, and what a recovering country should learn from the pattern. It then applies the same test to Iran's own leaders, because the lesson is not partisan.

*Method.* We describe what he has said, what fact-checkers and journalists have found, and what supporters and critics argue. We do not predict his motives. All items are as of 1 November 2026 and carry `[VERIFY]` where fast-moving.

## What the record shows

**The pattern.** Over many years, Trump has used superlative claims of knowledge and ability, including "nobody knows more about this than me", "nobody knew" and "believe me". Reporters have catalogued them. Examples cited in coverage include a 2015 claim to know more about ISIS than the generals, claims of unmatched military expertise, and claims to know more than scientists on several subjects. The fact-checker Daniel Dale of CNN has pointed out that "nobody knew" statements often cover things that many people did know. Slate decoded the habit years ago as a figure of speech in which "everybody" and "nobody" are loose.

**Specific cases.**
- He has repeatedly said that his 2000 book warned about Osama bin Laden before the 2001 attacks and that nobody listened. The Associated Press fact-checked this: the book was published more than 18 months before the attacks, not just before, and its mention of bin Laden was brief.
- In 2026, analysts noted that he described Iran's response to the US-Israeli operation as something "nobody" had expected, and CNN's analysis argued that many experts had in fact anticipated that kind of response `[VERIFY: original statement and date]`.
- Other widely reported examples include claims about the 2020 election, which courts and audits did not support, and statistics on crime and immigration that fact-checkers have called misleading `[VERIFY]`.

**What we could not confirm.** We found no verified statement in which he says he holds "secrets" known to no one else. A widely shared screenshot in May 2026, in which he supposedly wrote that nobody knows God better than him, was judged not authentic by Lead Stories and rated false by Media Bias Fact Check, while Snopes marked it "not verifiable". We do not attribute it to him. It is a useful reminder that false quotes travel as fast as real ones, which is a problem for anyone, including the person quoted.

## How supporters read it

Supporters argue that the style is hyperbole, a salesman's confidence that voters understand as a joke or an opening bid in a negotiation, and that it works. They say a leader who projects certainty reassures followers and unsettles adversaries, and they point out that fact-checkers sometimes treat figurative statements literally. Many also hold that he was right on matters elites dismissed, and that unusual self-confidence is what a disruptor needs.

## How critics read it

Critics argue that repeated unsupported claims erode public trust in facts, that "nobody knew" can disguise warnings that were ignored, and that claims of unique knowledge in wartime or in economic policy can short-circuit expert advice. They argue that leaders who treat their own judgment as beyond checking take larger and less reversible risks, and that supporters' "just hyperbole" defense is harder to make when the subject is war, health or elections.

Both sides agree on one fact: the claims are frequent and effective at getting attention.

## What recovering states should learn

1. **Separate the claim from the evidence.** Any official who says "I know something you don't" should be able to show it to an auditor, a court or a parliamentary committee, even if the public never sees the details.
2. **Build institutions that check leaders.** Independent statistics offices, courts, inspectors general, free press and legislative oversight exist to ask "how do you know?"
3. **Protect the fact-checkers.** Fact-checking itself should be open to challenge, and the checkers' methods published.
4. **Write the rule into the constitution.** No leader, however popular, should hold a monopoly on classification, statistics or "secret" information relevant to public safety.
5. **Do not copy the style.** A recovery depends on credibility, and a credibility built on boasting is easily lost.

## The same test for Iran

Iran's system has long rested on claims of unique authority: religious authority, security knowledge, and the final word on what is permitted. State media and officials have made claims that independent analysts have rejected, including inflation figures, casualty counts, and the scale of war damage `[VERIFY]`. The principle in this chapter applies to every leader of every country, our friends and rivals alike: **nobody's claim to know is a substitute for evidence anyone can check.**

## Objections and Honest Limits

*"This chapter targets one politician."* It uses one well-documented case to illustrate a general pattern, and it ends by applying the same test at home. Readers who prefer another example can substitute one; the method is unchanged.

*"Fact-checkers have biases."* Some do, which is why this chapter relies on named outlets, quotes few lines and flags uncertain items.

*"Confidence is part of leadership."* Agreed. The issue is not confidence but unfalsifiable confidence.

# Chapter 10 — Foreign Hands: Influence Operations in a Weakened Iran

A weakened state is a marketplace. Neighbors and great powers will compete to secure their own interests during any recovery, through trade, finance, media, border politics, proxies and propaganda. This chapter examines five influences that Iranians frequently raise: China, Turkey, the Arab states, Pakistan and India. We analyze **governments and policies, not peoples.** Iranians have deep and mostly friendly relations with the citizens of all five nations, and the coalition seeks good relations with all five states on equal terms.

**Method note.** For each actor we separate *documented* behavior, *plausible risks* and *allegations we cannot verify*. Many popular claims about "influence operations" are rumor; a credible book must say so.

## China

- **Documented/likely:** A long-term economic and strategic cooperation agreement reported in 2021, with figures that were never fully published `[VERIFY]`; large purchases of discounted Iranian oil through intermediaries, which gives Beijing leverage over a sanctioned seller; sales of electronics, surveillance and telecom equipment.
- **Risks for recovery:** Debt and contract terms that favor Chinese firms; surveillance technology that entrenches control; a dependency on a single buyer.
- **Our position:** Welcome Chinese investment under open, public contracts; refuse secret terms and technology that enables mass surveillance.

## Turkey

- **Documented/likely:** Deep trade and tourism links; Turkey as a major destination for Iranian emigrants, students and investors; real-estate purchases by Iranians; competition and cooperation over regional influence and water; cultural and media connections with Turkic-language communities in Iran.
- **Risks:** Identity politics that treat any one language community as a lever against national unity; economic leverage through border trade.
- **Our position:** Strong trade and cultural ties; no external patronage of any domestic community; shared management of rivers and borders.

## The Arab states, especially the Gulf

- **Documented/likely:** Long rivalry with Saudi Arabia and the UAE; disputes over the islands of Abu Musa and the Tunbs; Gulf money as a possible reconstruction source, tempered by reluctance to strengthen a rival; Arabic-language media that covers Iran and speaks to Iran's Arab communities.
- **Risks:** Funding strings; support for particular factions; use of Khuzestan's grievances as a political lever. Equally, Iranian-backed armed groups in the Arab world remain a source of regional resentment.
- **Our position:** A regional security pact with confidence-building measures, an end to the funding of foreign militias, and recognition that Iran's Arab citizens are Iranians first and deserve full rights and investment.

## Pakistan

- **Documented/likely:** A long, porous border through Baluchestan; cross-border militancy, smuggling and kidnapping; shared Sunni-Shia sensitivities; trade in fuel and goods; the movement of Afghan refugees.
- **Risks:** Militant groups exploiting a security vacuum; competing intelligence services; the rise of sectarian violence.
- **Our position:** Joint border management, a development plan for Baluchestan with local ownership, and zero tolerance for armed groups on either side.

## India

- **Documented/likely:** Chabahar port and connectivity projects; the North–South transport corridor; historical energy purchases; a balancing act between Washington, Tehran and the Gulf.
- **Risks:** Strategic projects stalled by sanctions; disappointments in delivery; competition with China over ports.
- **Our position:** Revive connectivity projects with transparent contracts and local hiring.

## Other actors

Russia, the United States, Israel, the European Union and Central Asian states are also players. They deserve their own chapters, to be added later.

## Defending against influence operations

1. **Transparency law.** Every foreign contract, loan and donation published; foreign agents registered; no secret annexes.
2. **Diversification.** No single country should hold more than a set share of trade, debt or technology.
3. **Media literacy and open press.** The best defense against propaganda is a free press that can expose it, and a public that can check claims.
4. **Cyber resilience.** Independent audits of telecom and surveillance technology.
5. **Regional diplomacy.** A standing forum of neighbors on water, borders, refugees and trade.
6. **Domestic fairness.** Foreign actors can only exploit grievances that exist. Treat minorities and border provinces as equals, and the lever disappears.

## Objections and Honest Limits

*"This chapter is too soft on regional rivals."* The coalition's goal is a stable neighborhood, not new hostility.

*"This chapter is too hard on China, Turkey or others."* We describe interests and risks; we do not accuse any people. Where evidence is thin, we say so.

*"Influence is just normal diplomacy."* Often true. The line we draw is secrecy, coercion and the buying of domestic factions.

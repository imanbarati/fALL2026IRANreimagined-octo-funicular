# Research method and chronology

## Why begin in 1984?
The Islamic Republic was founded in 1979 and the Iran–Iraq War began in 1980. Beginning in 1984 does not imply that the system began then. It selects a period when the new state had survived its first revolutionary years and was consolidating wartime institutions. The revolution, hostage crisis, early purges and the 1980–83 war remain essential context for a future prequel.

## What is meant by “the regime”?
The term refers to the constitutional and informal power system centred on the Supreme Leader and associated institutions: Guardian Council, Assembly of Experts, judiciary, intelligence and security agencies, Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC), Basij, state broadcasting, and economic foundations or companies linked to public and security elites. It does not mean every public employee, cleric, voter or Iranian Muslim.

Iran also contains elected institutions, competing factions, professional bureaucracies, local authorities and a diverse society. A useful history must explain both constitutional hierarchy and conflict within the system.

## Five dimensions of endurance
- **Legitimacy:** whether people regard rulers and procedures as rightful.
- **Coercive capacity:** ability to monitor, arrest, intimidate and use force.
- **Fiscal capacity:** ability to collect revenue, pay personnel and fund patronage.
- **Organizational reach:** networks inside workplaces, neighbourhoods, universities, religious institutions and the economy.
- **Administrative competence:** ability to provide reliable services and implement policy.

A decline in one dimension does not automatically produce decline in all five. A system may become less popular but more coercive, richer in protected networks but poorer in public services, or internally divided while powerful actors still share an interest in preserving the structure.

## Chronology: turning points, not an exhaustive event list
- **1984–1988:** wartime mobilization; expansion of IRGC and Basij; restrictions on opposition; 1988 mass executions of political prisoners.
- **1988–1989:** ceasefire with Iraq; succession after Khomeini's death; constitutional revision and selection of Ali Khamenei.
- **1989–1997:** reconstruction under Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani; economic opening within political limits; growth of technocratic and security-linked networks.
- **1997–2005:** Mohammad Khatami's election; civil-society and press opening; 1999 student protests; institutional vetoes and closures.
- **2005–2013:** Mahmoud Ahmadinejad presidency; contested privatization; 2009 Green Movement; sanctions escalation; nuclear dispute.
- **2013–2016:** Hassan Rouhani election; JCPOA negotiated in 2015; Implementation Day in January 2016 after IAEA verification of key steps.
- **2018–2021:** US withdrawal from JCPOA; renewed sanctions; Iran progressively exceeds nuclear limits; 2019 protests and lethal crackdown; COVID-19; killing of Qassem Soleimani in January 2020.
- **2021–2025:** Ebrahim Raisi presidency; 2022 Woman, Life, Freedom uprising after Jina Mahsa Amini's death in custody; expanded executions and pressure on civil society; regional conflict intensifies.
- **2025–2026:** June 2025 hostilities; renewed nuclear crisis and sanctions; protests beginning December 2025; US-Israeli strikes from February 2026; succession and continuing conflict as described by contemporaneous parliamentary and UN reporting.

## 2026 caution
The UK House of Commons Library briefing of 27 August 2026 states that US and Israeli strikes began on 28 February 2026, Ali Khamenei was killed on 29 February, and Mojtaba Khamenei was selected as successor. The UN Fact-Finding Mission's September 2026 report addresses protests beginning 28 December 2025 and hostilities beginning 28 February 2026. These extraordinary claims should be checked against primary statements and independent corroboration before adding tactical detail or casualty totals.

## Method and source hierarchy
Prioritize primary legal texts, UN and IAEA documents, official statistical agencies, contemporaneous parliamentary research, peer-reviewed scholarship and established human-rights investigations. A government statement is evidence of what a government said, not automatic proof that the claim is true. Advocacy organizations can provide valuable documentation, but their methods and access limits should be stated. Social-media posts and unattributed screenshots are leads, not evidence.

For each event ask: What happened and when? Which institution had authority? What is documented and what remains contested? Who bore the costs? How did the event alter the balance among elected bodies, unelected institutions, security organizations and society? What evidence would allow a future historian to revise the account?

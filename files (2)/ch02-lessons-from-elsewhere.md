# Chapter 2 — Lessons From Elsewhere

No country rebuilds from scratch. Others have walked this road, some well and some badly. This chapter draws on two bodies of experience: how states collapse, and how they recover.

## How states break: five cases

Our colleagues' earlier research on Iran's possible collapse compared five historical cases, and their outlines are worth restating because they show patterns recovery planners must anticipate. The figures below are drawn from that earlier work and are marked for verification.

- **Yugoslavia (1989–95).** Debt and austerity, then rival republics, then an army split along ethnic lines. Lesson: when the army fragments, politics becomes militia.
- **Lebanon (2019–).** A banking collapse and multiple exchange rates destroyed trust in all financial institutions; parallel economies grew `[VERIFY]`. Lesson: money is a political institution.
- **Syria (2011–2024).** A prolonged war and sanctions shrank the economy dramatically; an illicit economy became the most valuable sector `[VERIFY]`. Lesson: a hollowed-out state can survive for years but only at the price of its people.
- **Venezuela (2013–).** A petrostate refused to cut spending when oil fell, printed money, and watched roughly a quarter of its people emigrate `[VERIFY]`. Lesson: revenue dependence plus denial equals slow collapse.
- **The Soviet Union (1985–91).** Elite splits and half-hearted reform ended in dissolution. Lesson: ideology fails before institutions do.

Across these cases the same three-phase cascade repeats: **economic shock, institutional fragmentation, then either violent breakup, managed decay with mass emigration, or negotiated transition.** The coalition's goal is to make sure Iran takes the third path.

## How states recover: lessons to borrow

- **Post-war Germany and Japan.** Recovery paired external financing with domestic institutions: independent courts, a trusted currency, and free universities. The money mattered, but trust mattered more.
- **Poland and the Baltics after 1989.** Fast, visible reforms in the first year, a new currency, anti-corruption bodies, and a promise of belonging to something larger.
- **South Africa.** A transition that avoided civil war by negotiating, with a truth process that named crimes without destroying the country.
- **Chile, Spain, Portugal.** Peaceful transfers from authoritarian rule through pacts, constitutions and elections.
- **Iraq after 2003, and Libya.** The negative lesson: dissolving the old order wholesale, without replacing its functions, creates a vacuum filled by militias and foreign patrons.
- **Iran's own 1989–97 "reconstruction" era.** Scholarship describes a swift elite consensus around rebuilding after the Iran–Iraq war, major economic reforms undertaken, and a stalled reorientation amid political opposition and financial pressure. The lesson: rebuilding the roads without opening the politics repeats the same cycle.

## Five rules we take from history

1. **Do not leave a vacuum.** Keep the functions of the state running (pensions, water, courts, schools) while changing who runs them.
2. **Pay the security forces, legally and transparently.** Unpaid armed men are the most dangerous people in any transition.
3. **Stabilize money first.** A trusted currency is the first reform people feel.
4. **Reward early.** Visible wins in the first months buy patience for hard reforms.
5. **Hold elections only after the rules are fair.** Fast votes can entrench the strongest faction, which in Iran today may be the most armed one.

## Objections and Honest Limits

*"Iran is not Poland or Germany."* True. Iran's size, geography, ethnic diversity, security structure and resource base are unique, and every analogy is a hint, not a blueprint.

*"The collapse scenarios are alarmist."* They are risks, not forecasts. We include them because planners who only plan for success are surprised by failure.

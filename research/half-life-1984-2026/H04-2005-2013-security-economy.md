# H04 — 2005–2013: Securitization, oil wealth and pseudo-privatization

## A change in political direction
Mahmoud Ahmadinejad's election in 2005 brought a more confrontational political style and a stronger emphasis on revolutionary identity. High oil revenues during parts of the period gave the state significant resources. Revenue abundance did not automatically translate into diversified growth, stable institutions or broad-based accountability. Public spending, subsidies and patronage could ease pressure temporarily while leaving structural weaknesses unresolved.

## The economic state behind the slogans
A central development was the changing relationship between the formal state and parastatal organizations. A 2006 directive associated with the Supreme Leader allowed the transfer of government-owned enterprises to public, non-governmental entities and other bodies. Research by Kevan Harris describes how privatization could shift ownership to foundations, pension funds, cooperatives, banks and military-linked contractors rather than independent competitive firms. This is often described as pseudo-privatization: a change in legal ownership that does not necessarily create open competition or independent private capital.

The IRGC's economic role should be examined through contracts, corporate records, public procurement, infrastructure projects and ownership links. It is misleading to treat it as a single company or a perfectly unified actor. It is equally misleading to ignore the political consequences of a security institution becoming a major economic participant while its commercial activities are difficult for the public to scrutinize.

## 2009: the legitimacy crisis in the street
The disputed 2009 presidential election triggered the Green Movement. Large demonstrations challenged the official result and demanded accountability. The authorities rejected calls for a new election and used arrests, violence, censorship and pressure on opposition leaders to suppress the movement. The protest wave demonstrated both the capacity of citizens to mobilize and the state's capacity to control public space.

The episode weakened the credibility of elections for many citizens, pushed some activists into exile or silence, and reinforced the importance of security institutions in managing political conflict. It also showed that the state could survive a major legitimacy crisis without resolving the grievances that produced it.

## Sanctions and the nuclear dispute
From 2006 onward, UN Security Council resolutions imposed restrictions connected to Iran's nuclear activities. US and European sanctions expanded over time, affecting finance, energy exports, investment and trade. Sanctions had external causes and domestic consequences. They increased the value of opaque trade networks and intermediaries, raised the cost of ordinary transactions and created opportunities for actors with privileged access.

Sanctions were not the sole cause of Iran's economic problems, nor were they harmless pressure on the leadership. Their effects reached households, businesses, medical supply chains and public finances. State policy shaped who absorbed those costs.

## 2011–2013: pressure compounds
By the end of Ahmadinejad's presidency, inflation, currency instability, international isolation and factional conflict had become acute political issues. The economy was exposed to the combined effects of sanctions, oil dependence, weak governance and policy uncertainty. The 2013 election of Hassan Rouhani reflected public demand for a less confrontational approach and relief from the economic burden.

## What this period teaches
The period linked three mechanisms: security institutions gained economic reach; coercive responses to electoral protest deepened distrust; and sanctions reinforced opaque economic channels. None was inevitable. Transparent procurement, independent auditing and a negotiated nuclear settlement were possible alternatives, but required political choices and institutional constraints that were weak.

## Objections and honest limits
Sanctions were imposed by foreign governments and should be assessed under international law and by their humanitarian consequences. The nuclear dispute involved genuine verification questions. Nor should every state-linked company be presumed corrupt. The argument concerns weak transparency and conflicts of interest, not an unsupported claim about every contract or individual.

## What we will do / what readers can do
Preserve privatization and procurement records; build public ownership maps of major firms; and assess sanctions policy by measurable effects on both state capacity and civilian welfare.

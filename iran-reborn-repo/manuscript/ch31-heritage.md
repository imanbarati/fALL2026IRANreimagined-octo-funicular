# Chapter 31 — Heritage Destroyed and Heritage Saved

Iran’s cultural heritage is not a luxury for peacetime. It is part of the national balance sheet: Persepolis and Pasargadae, the bazaars and bridges of Isfahan, the mosques and madrasas of every city, the archaeological layers that run from Elamite to Qajar, the living traditions of music, craft, and language. War, neglect, looting, and ideological hostility have all taken their toll. A recovery that rebuilds roads and power plants while letting the material and living heritage decay is a recovery that impoverishes the future.

## The damage and the risk

Conflict has damaged or threatened sites through direct hits, secondary effects (fire, collapse, abandonment), and the breakdown of site protection. Decades of underfunding, poorly controlled development, and, in some periods, official indifference or hostility to pre-Islamic or non-conforming heritage have already eroded the stock. Looting and the illicit antiquities trade thrive when security collapses. The diaspora has preserved manuscripts, photographs, and memory, but physical sites cannot be shipped abroad.

At the same time, heritage is a source of soft power, tourism revenue, local identity, and intergenerational continuity. Destroying or neglecting it is a form of self-impoverishment.

## Principles

1. **Inventory first.** A rapid, transparent national inventory of damaged, at-risk, and intact heritage — movable and immovable, tangible and living — is the foundation of any policy. Without it, resources will be misallocated and claims of loss will remain contested.
2. **Protection is emergency work.** In the first hundred days, securing major sites against further damage, looting, and uncontrolled access is as important as securing power plants and hospitals.
3. **Restoration over erasure.** Ideological campaigns against pre-Islamic, non-Shi’a, or “Westernizing” heritage must end. Conservation follows professional standards, not political fashion.
4. **Living heritage matters.** Crafts, music, languages, and performance traditions are not museum pieces. Support for practitioners and transmission to the young is part of recovery.
5. **International cooperation without loss of ownership.** UNESCO, ICOMOS, and bilateral partners can supply expertise and funding. Ownership and final decisions remain Iranian. Diaspora museums and collections should be treated as partners in documentation and, where appropriate, temporary or permanent loans, not as rivals.

## Practical steps

- Establish an emergency heritage task force with authority to secure sites, coordinate with security forces, and halt destructive development.
- Publish a public damage and risk register, updated regularly.
- Prioritize stabilization of the most significant and most threatened monuments and urban fabrics.
- Restart professional training and employment for conservators, archaeologists, and craft masters; invite diaspora specialists for short missions.
- Integrate heritage into reconstruction planning so that new infrastructure does not destroy what remains.
- Support community-level living heritage initiatives as part of local recovery funds.

## Objections and Honest Limits

*"Heritage is secondary to food and electricity."* In the absolute emergency, yes. Within weeks, the two tracks can and must run together. Heritage sites that are left unsecured become sources of further loss and of conflict over responsibility.

*"Some sites represent the old regime or foreign influence."* Professional conservation distinguishes between historical layers and current political endorsement. Erasing history does not strengthen the present.

*"International help means foreign control."* Transparent contracts, Iranian leadership of teams, and public reporting prevent capture. Isolation has already produced loss.

## What We Will Do / What You Can Do

We will treat cultural heritage as a core recovery asset, not an afterthought. We will maintain links to professional networks and to diaspora documentation efforts.

If you work in heritage or related fields: document damage, secure what you can, and prepare professional plans that can be funded quickly. If you are a local official: treat major sites as critical infrastructure for security and future revenue. If you are in the diaspora: share documentation, expertise, and, where appropriate, temporary custody arrangements that keep material accessible and legally clear. If you design reconstruction packages: include a heritage stabilization line with professional standards and local employment.

What is destroyed cannot be fully recovered. What is saved becomes part of the new Iran’s capital — cultural, economic, and moral.

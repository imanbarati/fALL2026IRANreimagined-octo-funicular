# H05 — 2013–2021: Nuclear diplomacy, the JCPOA and the return of maximum pressure

## The promise of a negotiated opening
Hassan Rouhani's 2013 election brought a mandate to reduce Iran's international isolation and improve economic conditions. Negotiations with the P5+1—China, France, Germany, Russia, the United Kingdom and the United States—produced the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA) on 14 July 2015. The agreement placed limits on parts of Iran's nuclear programme and established monitoring arrangements in exchange for sanctions relief.

The UN Security Council endorsed the agreement through Resolution 2231. Implementation Day occurred on 16 January 2016 after the IAEA reported that Iran had completed key nuclear-related steps. Negotiated limits and verification were not hypothetical: they were implemented for a period and monitored by an international agency.

## The limits of the deal
The JCPOA was a nuclear agreement, not a comprehensive settlement of Iran's regional policy, ballistic missile programme, human-rights record or domestic constitutional structure. Supporters argued that it constrained nuclear risk and opened a path to economic normalization. Critics argued that it did not address other security concerns, had time-limited provisions and offered sanctions relief without resolving broader strategic competition.

The deal did not promise that Iran would become a liberal democracy, nor guarantee that foreign investment would arrive at the scale ordinary Iranians hoped. It did create a verifiable framework for nuclear restrictions.

## 2018: US withdrawal and maximum pressure
In May 2018, the Trump administration withdrew the United States from the JCPOA and reimposed sanctions. The other participants remained in the agreement, but the loss of US participation and the return of secondary sanctions sharply reduced the economic benefits available to Iran. Iran later began exceeding several JCPOA limits, presenting its actions as responses to the failure of other parties to deliver promised economic relief.

This sequence is central to a fair history. The US withdrawal was a policy choice by Washington; Iran's subsequent nuclear steps were choices by Tehran. Each altered incentives and reduced confidence in diplomacy.

## 2020–2021: escalation and institutional change
The US killing of IRGC Quds Force commander Qassem Soleimani in January 2020 intensified confrontation. Iran retaliated with missile strikes on bases hosting US forces in Iraq. The same month, Iranian forces mistakenly shot down Ukraine International Airlines Flight PS752, killing all 176 people aboard; the authorities initially denied responsibility before acknowledging the shootdown. The event became a major test of transparency and accountability.

COVID-19 placed further strain on health services and the economy. In 2021, Ebrahim Raisi won the presidency in an election in which many prominent potential candidates had been disqualified. The political field narrowed further, while negotiations to restore the JCPOA continued without a durable agreement.

## The nuclear file as a mirror of the state
The nuclear dispute exposed recurring features: concentration of strategic authority; use of national sovereignty as a powerful public argument; costs of opaque decision-making; and difficulty sustaining a diplomatic bargain when domestic and international incentives shift. It also exposed the limits of sanctions as a tool: pressure could impose costs, but did not automatically produce a stable agreement.

## Objections and honest limits
The JCPOA did not resolve every legitimate security concern, and IAEA verification cannot answer every question about intent. Conversely, sanctions are not a substitute for diplomacy and can impose severe civilian costs. A balanced history should separate nuclear compliance, safeguards obligations, political disputes and claims about weaponization.

## What we will do / what readers can do
Preserve the JCPOA, IAEA reports and Security Council documents; track the legal status of sanctions precisely; and insist that future nuclear policy be transparent, verifiable and subject to civilian oversight.

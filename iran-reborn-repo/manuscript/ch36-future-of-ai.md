# Chapter 36 — The Future of AI

Artificial intelligence is no longer a distant research topic. It is infrastructure, power demand, data governance, industrial strategy, and a new arena of geopolitical competition. For a recovering Iran the question is not whether AI will matter. It is whether the country will shape its use of the technology or be shaped by it — as a consumer of foreign systems, a site of surveillance tools left over from the old order, or a participant that builds domestic capacity under rules that protect citizens rather than control them.

The global picture in 2026 is clear. National AI strategies have proliferated. Compute, energy, and data have become the binding constraints. Data-centre electricity demand is rising sharply; grids and water supplies are already under strain in multiple regions. Governance has split into competing approaches: a United Nations–centred push for shared rules, a United States–centred emphasis on technological lead and national security, and a China–centred model of diffusion and capacity-building oriented toward the Global South. Open-weight models, proprietary frontier systems, and sovereign AI ambitions coexist uneasily. Energy and infrastructure, not model benchmarks alone, now define advantage.

Iran enters this landscape with mixed assets and heavy liabilities. It has produced a notable volume of AI research papers and retains technical talent, much of it in the diaspora. Domestic platforms have generated large user datasets. Successive governments have announced national AI plans, a National Artificial Intelligence Organization, and ambitions for technological self-reliance under sanctions. At the same time, the country has lived under extensive digital surveillance, internet blackouts, and the politicisation of technology. Brain drain has removed many of the people who would build competitive systems. Energy and grid reliability — already stressed — will determine whether large-scale compute can ever be hosted domestically. A recovery that simply inherits the old surveillance architecture under a new name will not be a recovery in the information domain.

## What AI can do for recovery

Used well, AI is a force multiplier for the tasks already outlined in this book.

- **Health and medicine (Chapter 29):** diagnostic support, supply-chain optimisation, epidemic surveillance, and tools that help remaining clinicians cover larger populations while returnees are recruited.
- **Energy and water (Chapters 12 and 28):** grid forecasting, demand management, predictive maintenance, and optimisation of scarce water and power resources.
- **Agriculture and scarcity (Chapter 11):** yield prediction, water-efficient irrigation guidance, and early warning for drought and pest pressures.
- **Education and universities (Chapter 30):** personalised tutoring in Persian and minority languages, research assistance, and tools that reduce the isolation of scholars who remain.
- **Public administration:** fraud detection in reconstruction spending, transparent procurement analytics, and citizen-facing services that reduce the need for intermediaries.
- **Language and culture (Chapter 8):** high-quality machine translation and speech tools that strengthen Persian and the other languages of Iran rather than erasing them.

None of these applications require Iran to win a global race to artificial general intelligence. They require reliable data, competent engineers, trustworthy institutions, and electricity that stays on.

## The risks that must be governed

Three risks stand out for a society emerging from repression and conflict.

First, **surveillance continuity**. The technical systems built for control do not disappear when political authority changes. Facial recognition, bulk interception, and domestic filtering infrastructure can be repurposed. Without explicit legal limits, independent oversight, and the dismantling or civilianisation of the most abusive tools, AI will simply make the old blackout-and-control model more efficient.

Second, **dependency and exclusion**. If advanced models, chips, and cloud capacity remain concentrated in a few foreign jurisdictions, Iran risks permanent technological dependence — or permanent exclusion if those jurisdictions treat it as a high-risk customer. Domestic capacity is therefore not only an industrial goal; it is a sovereignty goal. At the same time, closed national systems that cannot interoperate with global science will fall behind.

Third, **energy and environmental cost**. Large-scale training and inference consume power and water. A country already facing grid stress and water scarcity cannot treat data centres as an afterthought. AI strategy and energy strategy (Chapter 28) must be written together. Efficiency standards, renewable supply requirements, and transparent siting rules are not obstacles to progress; they are the conditions under which progress is sustainable.

## Principles for a recovering Iran

1. **Civilian purpose first.** Public AI investment should prioritise health, education, energy, agriculture, and transparent administration. Military and security applications must be subject to the same civilian oversight and legal limits that apply to other instruments of state power.
2. **Data rights and openness by default.** Personal data collected by the state or by large platforms must be governed by clear rules on consent, purpose limitation, and redress. Public-interest datasets (weather, agriculture, anonymised health statistics, official statistics) should be opened under licences that enable research and domestic industry.
3. **Compute and energy realism.** National ambitions must be matched to actual power, cooling, and grid capacity. Prefer efficient models, shared public compute for universities and startups, and co-location with renewable generation over prestige projects that cannot be powered.
4. **Talent circulation.** The diaspora is a strategic asset (Chapters 9, 30, 32). Dual appointments, remote collaboration, and short-term return programs for AI researchers and engineers should be designed early, without political loyalty tests.
5. **International engagement without capture.** Participate in global and regional governance forums. Seek compute and research partnerships that come with transparency and without exclusive political conditionality. Avoid locking the country’s future into any single external technological bloc.
6. **Reject the surveillance default.** The information-blackout architecture of the recent past (Chapter 24) must not be upgraded with AI. Independent oversight of any state AI systems used for security or public-order purposes is non-negotiable.

## Practical steps in the transition

**Emergency and early stabilisation:**

- Inventory existing state AI and surveillance systems; place the most intrusive under independent technical and legal review.
- Restore open academic and research access to global literature, models, and datasets (subject to ordinary export-control law, not political filtering).
- Stand up a temporary civilian AI coordination cell focused on health, energy, and reconstruction analytics, with published terms of reference.

**Stabilisation:**

- Enact a basic data-protection and algorithmic-accountability framework that covers both public and large private systems.
- Create a competitive public compute facility for universities and qualified startups, powered under the energy rules of Chapter 28.
- Launch targeted return and dual-affiliation programs for AI talent.
- Require transparency reporting for any AI system used in public decision-making that significantly affects individuals.

**Longer horizon:**

- Build domestic capability in applied AI for national priority domains rather than attempting to replicate every frontier model.
- Integrate AI literacy into education and public administration so that citizens and officials can use and question the tools.
- Participate in international scientific and governance processes from a position of domestic competence, not dependency.

## Objections and Honest Limits

*"We must achieve technological self-reliance at any cost."* Self-reliance that produces isolated, low-quality systems is not strength. Smart openness — domestic capacity plus selective international collaboration — is more durable.

*"AI will solve unemployment and inefficiency automatically."* AI can raise productivity; it can also concentrate gains and displace workers without complementary education, social policy, and competition rules. Recovery policy must include the human side.

*"Security requires unrestricted state AI."* Unrestricted state AI is how the information blackout became possible. Legal limits, oversight, and civilian purpose are the difference between a security tool and a permanent instrument of domestic control.

*"We are too poor to invest in AI."* Applied AI for health, energy, and administration can reduce costs and improve outcomes. Prestige frontier projects that cannot be powered or staffed are the luxury. Prioritisation is the discipline.

## What We Will Do / What You Can Do

We will treat AI as a cross-cutting recovery technology whose governance must match the principles of equal citizenship, transparency, and civilian primacy already set out in this book. We will maintain technical annexes on data-protection baselines, public-compute design, and oversight of state AI systems.

If you are a researcher or engineer inside Iran or in the diaspora: document capabilities and barriers; prepare concrete collaboration and return offers. If you design transitional institutions: write the data and AI rules early, before systems harden. If you control reconstruction finance: fund applied public-interest AI and the energy to power it, under transparency conditions. If you are a citizen: demand that the tools used to govern you are themselves governed.

The future of AI in Iran will be decided by institutions, electricity, talent, and law — not by slogans about ranking or self-reliance. A recovery that gets those four right can use the technology to heal and to build. A recovery that gets them wrong will simply automate the failures of the past.

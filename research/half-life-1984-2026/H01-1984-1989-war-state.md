# H01 — 1984–1989: The war state becomes a governing system

## The front and the rear
By 1984, the Iran–Iraq War had entered a phase in which the Islamic Republic's survival and the prosecution of the war were intertwined. The state mobilized conscripts, volunteers, religious networks, revolutionary institutions and a national language of sacrifice. Iraq's invasion created a real national emergency; defending Iranian territory was not identical to endorsing every domestic policy of the government. A country can resist invasion while its rulers use wartime conditions to narrow political life.

The IRGC and Basij expanded during the war. Their wartime roles gave them organizational experience, social networks, public standing among some constituencies and a claim to a permanent place in national security. After the war, these assets did not simply disappear. They became part of the institutional inheritance of the Islamic Republic. The war therefore helped produce a security architecture that could operate inside society as well as defend the state.

## A constitutional order with a supervisory ceiling
The 1979 constitution had already created a hybrid system: elected bodies existed, but clerical supervisory institutions and the doctrine of velayat-e faqih placed higher authority above ordinary electoral competition. The 1984 parliamentary electoral law and the candidate-screening system illustrate how formal elections operated within boundaries shaped by unelected bodies and security-linked institutions.

The system was not monolithic. Conservatives and left-Islamists disagreed over economic policy, the role of the state and the conduct of the war. But disagreement took place inside a framework that excluded many secular, liberal, leftist and other opposition currents. The state's ability to define legitimate participation was itself a source of power.

## 1988: ceasefire and the prison executions
The 1988 ceasefire ended the war with Iraq without a decisive victory for either side. The same year, thousands of imprisoned political opponents were executed following proceedings widely condemned by human-rights organizations as summary and grossly unfair. The exact total remains disputed because the authorities have not provided a full public accounting and many records remain inaccessible. The scale, secrecy, selection of prisoners and absence of transparent due process make this a central episode in any history of state violence.

A responsible account must distinguish established testimony, named victims, family accounts, official denials and estimates. Uncertainty about the exact number does not erase the documented pattern or the need for truth, identification of victims, access to burial records and accountability.

## 1989: succession and constitutional redesign
Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini died in June 1989. Ali Khamenei was selected as Supreme Leader, and constitutional amendments strengthened the executive and clarified the leadership structure. The post-Khomeini state was not simply the continuation of a charismatic revolutionary moment. It became a system whose survival depended increasingly on offices, appointments, security bodies and elite bargains.

The Expediency Discernment Council, established in 1988 to resolve disputes between parliament and the Guardian Council, became another component of this structure. Institutions could manage conflicts among insiders without opening the system to unrestricted competition.

## What this period left behind
The period created four durable legacies: a militarized political culture; security organizations with battlefield and domestic roles; a constitutional hierarchy limiting electoral sovereignty; and a history of mass violence for which public accountability remained absent. These legacies help explain later endurance, but they do not make later outcomes inevitable. Iranian society remained diverse, politically active and capable of challenging the state.

## Objections and honest limits
Wartime mobilization cannot be reduced to regime manipulation. Many Iranians volunteered from patriotism, family duty, religious conviction or defence of their homes. Nor should the IRGC's wartime history be treated as proof that every later action by every member was identical. The analytical point is institutional: wartime organizations acquired durable resources and political standing that outlasted the emergency.

## What we will do / what readers can do
Preserve prison, cemetery, military and parliamentary records; support independent research into the 1988 executions; and keep the distinction between national defence and the political interests of rulers visible in public debate.

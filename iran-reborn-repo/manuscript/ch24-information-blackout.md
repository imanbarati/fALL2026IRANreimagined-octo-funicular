# Chapter 24 — The Information Blackout

A nation that cannot speak to itself cannot recover. When the lights of the network go dark, hospitals lose coordination, families lose the missing, markets lose price signals, and the outside world loses the evidence of what is being done. Iran has lived this more than once. The longest national-scale blackout in its modern history ran from 8 January 2026 through late May 2026 — roughly 88 days and more than 2,000 hours of near-total isolation for a population of about ninety million. It began amid protests over living costs and continued through the opening months of the six-month war. Monitors recorded connectivity collapsing to a few percent of normal levels; domestic services were throttled or whitelist-only; Starlink and other work-arounds were actively jammed or priced out of reach. Earlier episodes — November 2019, the Woman Life Freedom movement of 2022, the June 2025 Twelve-Day War — had already taught the pattern. Each blackout bought time for force while erasing the public record.

We do not treat this as a technical footnote. An information blackout is a political and economic weapon. It multiplies every other form of damage: it hides killings, freezes remittances, collapses supply chains that run on messaging apps, and leaves doctors without the ability to locate blood or medicine. Recovery therefore begins with the restoration of a free, resilient, and accountable information space. Without it, every later reform — currency stabilization, truth commissions, private investment, reverse migration — operates in the dark.

## What the blackouts taught us

Three hard lessons emerge from the record.

First, the architecture has matured. Early shutdowns were crude BGP withdrawals. Later ones used the National Information Network (NIN) for selective connectivity: domestic traffic could be kept partially alive while international routes and major platforms were severed. Whitelisting of government-approved services and aggressive filtering of VPNs and satellite links became standard. The result is not total silence but controlled silence — enough domestic messaging to keep some commerce and propaganda running, not enough for independent journalism or cross-border coordination.

Second, the economic cost is immediate and measurable. Businesses that depend on international payments, logistics platforms, and customer messaging lose days or weeks of revenue. Informal markets for SIM cards, VPN access, and satellite bandwidth inflate prices by factors of five to twenty. The poorest households, already under pressure from currency collapse and food inflation, are cut off from diaspora support and from the information that might help them find work or aid.

Third, the human cost is irreversible for those who die or disappear while the cameras are offline. Families cannot publish photos of the missing. Hospitals cannot share blood inventories. Monitors outside the country receive only fragments smuggled by those who reach a border or a working Starlink terminal. When the network returns, the body count is already contested and the evidence trail is thinner.

These are not abstractions. They are the conditions under which any transitional authority will have to operate on day one.

## Principles for a free information space

We propose five non-negotiable principles.

1. **Continuity of civilian connectivity.** No transitional authority, and no successor government, may impose a nationwide blackout for political control. Targeted, time-limited restrictions on specific networks during active combat operations may be justified on narrow military grounds and must be published, reviewed by an independent technical panel, and lifted at the earliest safe moment. Blanket civilian cut-offs are banned.

2. **End of the whitelist regime.** The practice of routing only approved domestic services while blocking the open internet must end. The NIN may continue as a domestic backbone for resilience, but it must not be used as a filter that substitutes for the global network.

3. **Transparency of infrastructure.** Ownership and control of major internet exchange points, backbone providers, and filtering systems must be published. Any remaining state or quasi-state holdings in telecoms must be placed under independent trusteeship pending transparent privatization or restructuring that prevents concentration of power in former security organs.

4. **Protection of circumvention and journalism tools.** Import, sale, and use of VPNs, mesh networks, satellite terminals, and encryption tools must be legal. Journalists and human-rights monitors must have explicit legal protection for the tools they use to document events.

5. **Independent oversight.** A civilian Information Integrity Commission, staffed by engineers, journalists, and civil-society representatives (including diaspora expertise), must have statutory power to audit filtering, demand real-time traffic data during crises, and publish findings. Its budget and appointments must be insulated from day-to-day executive control.

## Immediate steps in the first hundred days

These steps align with the continuity logic of Chapter 4 and with the broader stabilization frameworks already circulating among opposition planners.

- Day 1–3: Publish a public order restoring full international connectivity and suspending political filtering. Release any detained network engineers, journalists, or VPN operators held solely for technical activity.
- Day 3–14: Stand up an emergency technical task force (domestic and diaspora engineers) to map remaining chokepoints, restore peering, and document residual filtering. Publish daily connectivity dashboards.
- Day 14–40: Begin legal review of the laws and decrees that authorized previous blackouts. Freeze any new procurement of deep-packet inspection or domestic filtering systems until the Information Integrity Commission is seated.
- Day 40–100: Open the process for licensing new independent internet service providers and for restructuring existing ones. Invite international technical assistance for resilience (undersea cable diversity, satellite backup, mesh community networks) under transparent contracts.

Parallel to these steps, a public archive of the blackout periods must be begun: timestamps of outages, official justifications, documented deaths and disappearances that occurred while the network was dark, and economic loss estimates. This archive is both a justice tool and a deterrent.

## Lessons from elsewhere

Post-conflict and post-authoritarian recoveries that treated information as infrastructure recovered faster. Liberia’s early investment in connectivity after its civil wars supported reconciliation processes and market recovery. Rwanda’s deliberate expansion of ICT access, whatever its other political constraints, created measurable gains in service delivery and private-sector formation. Conversely, prolonged information controls in other post-conflict settings have correlated with slower trust recovery and higher risk of renewed violence, because rumor fills the vacuum left by verified reporting.

The Forum on Information and Democracy and related policy work have codified practical recommendations: promote plurality of conflict-related information, reduce the algorithmic amplification of fear speech and disinformation, and enable independent scrutiny of both state and platform actions. These are not Western luxuries; they are operational requirements for any society trying to move from coercion to consent.

## Objections and Honest Limits

*"National security requires the ability to shut the network."* Narrow, published, time-limited measures against specific military targets are distinguishable from nation-wide civilian blackouts. The latter have repeatedly failed to stop protests while succeeding in hiding casualties and destroying economic value. A recovering Iran needs the opposite: maximum visibility of threats and maximum continuity of civilian life.

*"Opening the network will flood the country with foreign propaganda and disinformation."* Closed networks do not eliminate propaganda; they monopolize it. The remedy is plurality, fact-checking capacity in Persian and minority languages, media literacy, and platform accountability — not a return to the whitelist.

*"We lack the technical capacity to manage a fully open system."* Iran already possesses deep engineering talent, much of it in the diaspora. The blackout itself demonstrated sophisticated control systems; the same talent can be redirected to openness and resilience. International technical assistance can fill gaps under transparent, non-political terms.

*"Former security organs still control the physical infrastructure."* That is precisely why trusteeship, published ownership, and independent oversight are first-order priorities. Ignoring the problem leaves the blackout capability intact for the next crisis.

## What We Will Do / What You Can Do

We will maintain a living technical annex of recommended statutes, model contracts for telecom restructuring, and open-source tools for community connectivity. We will support the training of independent auditors and the creation of a public blackout archive.

If you are inside the country: document residual filtering, protect your own communications, and demand published connectivity metrics from any authority that claims to govern. If you are in the diaspora: contribute engineering expertise, fund independent Persian-language fact-checking, and keep pressure on international partners to treat open connectivity as a non-negotiable condition of reconstruction support. If you are a policymaker or donor: make full, verifiable restoration of the open internet a gating criterion for stabilization funding.

A recovery conducted in the dark is not a recovery. It is a pause before the next controlled silence. We choose the light.

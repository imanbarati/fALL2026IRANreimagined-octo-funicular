# Chapter 33 — Money from Abroad

Reconstruction will cost money that Iran does not currently have in liquid, unencumbered form. Estimates circulating in 2026 put the scale of needed recovery investment in the range of hundreds of billions of dollars; one widely cited figure for a comprehensive package has been on the order of $300 billion `[VERIFY]`. Gulf states have shown hesitation. Western and Asian partners will attach conditions. Private capital will demand rule of law and enforceable contracts. The question is not whether external resources will be part of the picture; it is under what terms, with what sequencing, and with what safeguards against the capture and corruption that have marked previous resource inflows.

## The financing landscape

Possible sources include: released or newly accessible frozen assets; bilateral and multilateral official finance; export-credit and investment guarantees; private foreign direct investment; diaspora capital; and, over time, restored oil and gas revenues under transparent management. Each source has different speed, conditionality, and political price.

Sanctions relief is itself a sequencing problem. Premature or poorly conditioned relief can reward unreformed structures. Delayed relief can starve a fragile transition of oxygen. The same logic applies to reconstruction grants and loans.

## Principles

1. **Transparency as the entry price.** Beneficial ownership of contracting entities, public disclosure of major contracts, and independent audit of reconstruction funds are non-negotiable. Money that cannot be tracked will be stolen or diverted.
2. **Sequencing that rewards reform.** Disbursements should be tied to verifiable milestones: currency stabilization measures, ownership disclosure in key sectors, competition in previously monopolized markets, and basic rights protections. Front-loading without conditions recreates the old incentive structure.
3. **Diversification of sources.** Over-reliance on any single foreign patron — Gulf, Western, Chinese, or other — creates strategic vulnerability. A balanced portfolio reduces the risk that any one external actor can dictate terms.
4. **Domestic revenue mobilization.** External money is a bridge, not a permanent substitute. Tax reform, subsidy rationalization, and the recovery of oil and gas revenues under civilian, transparent control must run in parallel.
5. **Anti-corruption architecture from day one.** A reconstruction fund or equivalent vehicle should have an independent board, international observers where appropriate, published accounts, and real investigative capacity. Retroactive anti-corruption campaigns after the money is gone are less effective.

## Practical design

- Establish a National Reconstruction Fund (or equivalent) with a published charter, independent auditors, and a public dashboard of commitments, disbursements, and results.
- Negotiate sanctions relief in stages linked to concrete domestic steps, not to vague political promises.
- Create a one-stop, transparent window for foreign and diaspora investment with clear rules, not ad-hoc deal-making.
- Ring-fence essential humanitarian and service-continuity finance from more discretionary reconstruction finance so that basic needs are not held hostage to political negotiations.
- Publish a medium-term fiscal framework that shows how external inflows fit with domestic revenue and expenditure paths.

## Objections and Honest Limits

*"Conditions are an infringement of sovereignty."* Unconditional money that finances the same opaque networks is also an infringement — of the future of ordinary Iranians. Sovereignty includes the capacity to refuse terms that would recreate capture.

*"We need money now; conditionality causes delay."* Unconditional money that is stolen or wasted causes longer delay. Smart sequencing can move emergency funds quickly while holding larger investment packages to higher standards.

*"Gulf or Chinese money comes with fewer lectures."* It also comes with its own strategic interests and, often, lower transparency. Diversification and domestic rules are the protection.

## What We Will Do / What You Can Do

We will treat financing architecture as a core design problem of the transition, equal in importance to the political and security architecture. We will maintain model charters for reconstruction funds and conditionality matrices that can be adapted.

If you negotiate on behalf of a transitional authority: publish the terms you seek and the safeguards you offer. If you represent a potential funder: make transparency and competition hard conditions, not soft preferences. If you are a citizen or civil-society monitor: demand public dashboards and the right to audit. If you are in the diaspora with capital: invest through transparent vehicles and refuse side deals that undermine the rules.

Money from abroad can accelerate recovery. Money from abroad without rules will finance the next failure. The difference is institutional design, not the nationality of the payer.
